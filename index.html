Lesson 1

Know the controls

Tap a dot to learn what that control does. The R50 has one command dial, so many settings live on the touchscreen and the Q button. Learning these few controls is what makes manual shooting fast.

Simplified map for learning. Button positions are approximate, so match each name to the label printed on your camera.

The single dial, explained

In Av the dial sets aperture. In Tv it sets shutter speed. In P it shifts the shutter and aperture pair. In M it sets shutter speed; press the ▲ cross key once and the dial switches to aperture until you press it again or turn the camera off.

In P, Tv and Av the ▲ key opens exposure compensation instead. That is the brightness knob for the semi-auto modes.

The Q button is your shortcut menu

Press Q/SET while shooting and the screen shows the settings you change most: AF operation, AF area, drive, metering, white balance, picture style, image quality. Tap one, change it, press Q again. You will rarely need the full Menu while shooting.

Field assignment

Without looking at the screen, find these by feel: shutter button, main dial, ISO button, ▲ key, Q/SET. Then change ISO to 800 and back to Auto using only the ISO button and the dial. Repeat until it takes under five seconds.

Lesson 2

Exposure Lab

Three settings control how bright a photo is, and each one has a side effect you can see. Pick a scene, pick a lens, then change one setting at a time and watch the frame, the meter, and the notes.

Exposure level indicator, like the one in your viewfinder. Center is the camera's idea of correct.

What you are seeing

Mode
Smaller f-number = bigger opening = more light and more background blur.
Faster = less light and frozen motion. Slower = more light, blur, and shake.
Higher ISO = brighter, with more grain.

The exposure triangle

Aperture f/2.8 · f/8

The size of the opening in the lens. f/2.8 is a big hole and f/16 is a pinhole. Each full stop (f/2.8 → 4 → 5.6 → 8) cuts the light in half. Big opening gives a blurry background. Small opening keeps more of the scene sharp front to back.

Shutter speed 1/500 · 1/30

How long the sensor collects light. 1/500 is half as long as 1/250, so half the light. Fast freezes action. Slow shows motion blur, and below a certain speed your own hand shake shows up too.

ISO 100 · 6400

How much the camera amplifies the signal. ISO 200 is twice as bright as 100. It does not add light, it boosts what you have, so grain rises with it. On the R50, 100 to 3200 is clean, 6400 to 12800 is usable, above that is a last resort.

One stop means double or half the light. Open the aperture one stop and you can use a shutter one stop faster and get the same brightness. That trade is the whole game.

Field assignment

Put the 40mm on. Set Av, f/2.8, Auto ISO. Shoot a mug on a table from 2 feet away. Then shoot the same frame at f/5.6, f/11 and f/22. Compare the background on your phone. Then switch to Tv and photograph a moving ceiling fan at 1/15, 1/125 and 1/1000.

Crop factor in one sentence

The R50's sensor is smaller than full frame, so it sees only the middle of what a lens projects, which makes every lens act about 1.6 times longer. Your 40mm frames like a 64mm, and your 18-55 frames like a 29-88. The lens itself did not change; your sensor crops it.

Your T5 had the same size sensor, so both lenses will frame exactly as they did before.

Telephoto compression

Long lenses make background objects look bigger and closer to the subject, because you are standing farther back. That is why portraits at 85mm equivalent and up look flattering and why a stadium backdrop looms behind a player in a telephoto shot. Wide lenses do the opposite and exaggerate what is closest.

Read a lens name

Tap each part of your zoom's name. The label on your barrel may read IS II or IS STM, depending on which version came with your T5.

Field assignment

Stand in one spot in your yard. Shoot the same tree at 18mm, 35mm and 55mm without moving your feet. Then walk forward until the tree fills the frame at 18mm and compare it with the 55mm shot. Same size tree, very different background. That difference is perspective.

Lesson 4

Focus that hits

The R50's Dual Pixel CMOS AF II is its best feature. It can find and track eyes on people and animals across most of the frame. Your job is to choose how it behaves: once or continuously, and where it is allowed to look.

AF operation One-Shot or Servo

One-Shot focuses once when you half-press and locks. Use it for still subjects: food, landscapes, posed portraits. You can focus, then recompose while holding the half-press.

Servo keeps refocusing while you hold the half-press. Use it for anything that moves: sports, dogs, kids, a band on stage.

Subject detection

Set the subject to detect to People, Animals or Vehicles, and turn eye detection on. With Whole Area AF the camera finds the subject on its own. Tap the screen to pick a different person.

Manual focus aid: switch the lens to MF and turn on focus peaking. Sharp edges light up in color.

Set Servo, Whole Area AF, Animals, eye detection on, high-speed burst. Have someone toss a ball for the dogs and hold the half-press as they run toward you. Count how many frames out of twenty have a sharp eye. Then try 1-point AF and compare the hit rate.

Lesson 5

The mode dial

Each mode splits the work between you and the camera. Tap a mode to see who controls what. P, Tv, Av and M are the creative modes, and they are where you will learn.

The path most people take

Week 1 to 2: Av. You choose depth of field, the camera handles the rest. Learn exposure compensation with the ▲ key.

Week 3: Tv. For sports and pets. Learn what shutter speed freezes what.

Week 4 and on: M with Auto ISO. You lock aperture and shutter, the camera picks ISO. This is how most action and event shooters work. One R50 quirk makes it extra useful: in Av with Auto ISO you cannot set a minimum shutter speed, so the camera may pick a speed too slow for moving subjects. M with Auto ISO removes that risk.

Field assignment

Shoot one full afternoon in Av only. Every time a photo is too bright or too dark, fix it with exposure compensation instead of switching modes. Note which scenes fooled the meter: bright skies, white walls, dark rooms.

Quick fixes

Blurry subject, sharp background: shutter too slow for the motion. Go faster.

Everything soft: camera shake. Faster shutter, use the IS zoom, or brace yourself.

Wrong thing in focus: narrow the AF area or tap the subject on screen.

Too dark, can't slow down: open the aperture, then raise ISO.

Sky blown out: exposure compensation −1, or shoot RAW and recover.

Stripes under gym lights: switch from electronic shutter to electronic first-curtain.
